# Ultra-Processed People: Facts for a Scrollytelling Experience
### Based on *Ultra-Processed People: The Science Behind Food That Isn't Food* by Dr. Chris van Tulleken (2023)

---

## Overview

Dr. Chris van Tulleken — infectious disease doctor, molecular virologist, and BBC broadcaster — spent a month eating only ultra-processed food as a personal experiment. What followed changed his understanding of modern disease. This document collects the key facts, statistics, and arguments from his book and related peer-reviewed research, structured for a scrollytelling experience.

---

## 1. What Is Ultra-Processed Food (UPF)?

- **The NOVA classification** divides all food into four groups. Group 4 — UPF — is defined as *"formulations of ingredients, mostly of exclusive industrial use and made by a series of industrial processes."*
- **The kitchen test**: If an ingredient on a food packet isn't something you would normally find in a home kitchen, it's a UPF.
- **Examples of UPF ingredients** include modified starches, synthetic emulsifiers, artificial flavourings, gums, colour additives, and preservatives — none of which exist in nature.
- **UPF is not just "junk food."** Many products marketed as healthy — protein bars, diet yoghurts, plant-based meats, baby formula — meet the definition of UPF.
- **Key distinction**: Humans have processed food for millennia (drying, fermenting, salting). UPF is categorically different — it uses industrial techniques unavailable throughout most of human history.

> *"It's not food. It's an industrially produced edible substance."* — Chris van Tulleken

---

## 2. How Much UPF Are We Eating?

- In the **United States and United Kingdom**, over **50–60% of the average adult diet** now consists of UPF.
- For **younger people, lower-income groups, and residents of deprived areas**, that figure can reach **up to 80%** of total calorie intake.
- Americans currently derive approximately **57% of their calories from UPF**.
- The shift is global. Since the 1980s in Brazil, the cheapness and convenience of UPF has replaced traditional whole-food diets, driving dramatic rises in obesity across lower-income nations.
- **For the first time in human history**, the majority of calories consumed by many populations come from an entirely novel category of industrial substances.

## 3. The Health Toll — The Numbers

### Mortality & Cardiovascular Disease
- A landmark **BMJ umbrella review (2024)** analysed 9,888,373 participants across 45 pooled analyses and found UPF directly associated with **32 adverse health outcomes**.
- **50% increased risk** of death from cardiovascular disease (convincing Class I evidence).
- **66% higher risk** of dying from heart disease (highly suggestive Class II evidence).
- **21% increased risk** of all-cause mortality — dying from any cause.

### Mental Health
- **48–53% increased risk** of anxiety and common mental disorders (Class I evidence).
- **22% elevated risk** of depression (Class II evidence).
- UPF consumption affects dopamine reward pathways in the brain, driving cravings and disordered eating behaviour similar to patterns seen in substance addiction.

### Metabolic & Hormonal
- **40–66% higher risk** of obesity.
- **12% increased risk** of developing type 2 diabetes (per additional 10% of diet comprised of UPF — Class I dose-response evidence).
- **40% higher risk** of sleep disturbances.

### Cancer
- A **10% increase** in the proportion of UPF in the diet is associated with a **13% increase** in overall cancer risk.
- Elevated risks established for **colorectal cancer** (30% higher risk), **breast cancer** (11% higher), and **pancreatic cancer** (49% higher).
- Researchers from the **International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC/WHO)** confirmed UPF is linked to cancer and cardiometabolic multimorbidity in a 2023 multinational study.
- Replacing just **10% of UPF** with minimally processed food is associated with reduced risk across multiple cancers including colorectal, breast, oesophageal, and liver cancers.

### The Broadest Verdict (2025)
- The world's largest scientific review to date, evaluated by **43 top experts**, found UPF is harmful to **every major human organ system**.
- Of 104 long-term studies reviewed, **92 reported elevated risks** for at least one chronic disease or increased all-cause mortality.

## 4. How UPF Hijacks the Body

### Texture & Satiation
- UPF is typically **soft and dry** — engineered to be easy to eat quickly and in large quantities before satiation kicks in.
- Its dryness, designed for long shelf life, also makes UPF **more calorie-dense per gram** than equivalent whole foods.

### The Missing Micronutrients Loop
- Intensive industrial processing **destroys the micronutrient content** of food, stripping away smell and complex flavour.
- Manufacturers add **artificial flavourings** to compensate — but the body keeps searching for the missing real nutrients, driving continued consumption.

### Hunger Hormones
- During his month-long UPF experiment, van Tulleken experienced **hormone dysregulation** — his hunger signalling systems were disrupted, causing him to eat more even when calorically replete.
- This led to elevated cholesterol, systemic inflammation, and measurable health decline — all within 30 days.

### The Obesity Argument
- Van Tulleken argues UPF is a **greater driver of weight gain than sugar or lack of exercise**.
- Evidence cited includes a 2012 study showing **no significant difference in weight gain** between high- and low-carbohydrate diets — undermining the sugar-as-primary-cause hypothesis.
- Exercise, he argues, cannot compensate: when we exercise more, the body simply reallocates calories from other systems to maintain a **constant total expenditure**. The real lever is appetite regulation — which UPF disrupts.
- He frames obesity not as a personal failing, but as a **"commerciogenic disease"** — one manufactured by a largely unregulated industry.

## 5. Engineered for Addiction

- UPF products are **"specifically engineered to drive excess consumption."**
- Industrial processing techniques such as **extrusion and moulding** create textures and forms with no analogue in nature, making these products uniquely compelling.
- Food companies intentionally formulate products to maximise **hyper-palatability** — the combination of fat, salt, sugar, and texture engineered to override normal satiation.
- Van Tulleken reframes UPF as an **addictive substance**, not merely unhealthy food, arguing this classification is key to understanding why individuals cannot simply "eat less."
- Brain imaging and behavioural evidence shows UPF alters **dopamine pathway responses**, mirroring patterns documented in substance use disorders.

> *"Ultra-processed foods are designed to be addictive, leading to overconsumption."*

---

## 6. The Regulatory Failure

- In the **United States, since 1997**, food manufacturers have been permitted to determine for themselves whether new additives are safe — a category known as **GRAS (Generally Recognized as Safe)**.
- This self-certification system means **hundreds of additives** enter the food supply with minimal independent safety evaluation.
- Many additives and chemicals previously used in food have since been banned, but novel substitutes continue to be introduced under the same inadequate framework.
- Van Tulleken warns that **antibiotic resistance** — one of the most serious global health threats — may be exacerbated by UPF, through additive chemicals and factory-farmed animal products: *"Another existential threat to human life caused by UPF but not mentioned on the packaging is antibiotic resistance."*
- The food industry has a structural incentive to **oppose regulation** and fund research that obscures harm — a dynamic van Tulleken compares to the tobacco industry's strategy of manufacturing scientific doubt.

---

## 7. The Environmental Cost

- The production of UPF's cheap commodity ingredients — principally **soy and corn** — drives large-scale **deforestation**, especially of tropical rainforests.
- Monocrops grown for UPF additives and factory-farmed animals consume vast swathes of land that could feed people directly.
- The energy-intensive mechanical processing of whole foods and the **global supply chains** required to source and combine ingredients from multiple countries contribute significantly to **greenhouse gas emissions**.
- Van Tulleken calls UPF the **"number one cause of environmental destruction"** — a claim that goes beyond its human health toll.

---

## 8. The Social Justice Dimension

- UPF is disproportionately consumed by **lower-income populations** and communities in deprived areas — not because of ignorance, but because it is often the **only available and affordable option**.
- The food industry deliberately targets **marketing at children and vulnerable communities**, normalising UPF as everyday food culture.
- The **Nestlé baby formula scandal** — in which formula was aggressively marketed to mothers in poor nations, contributing to thousands of infant deaths — is used by van Tulleken as a case study in industry harm and the importance of independent policy.
- For many people, **UPF is their entire food culture**. This makes individual dietary change insufficient without systemic intervention.

---

## 9. What Can Be Done?

- Van Tulleken stops short of prescribing a specific diet, but advocates for **reading food labels** carefully and identifying any ingredient that would not be found in a normal kitchen.
- He suggests a **"80% UPF experiment"** — intentionally eating an ultra-processed diet for a few days to feel its effects firsthand.
- At the policy level, he draws two lessons from historical food scandals:
  1. **Policymakers must not be funded by the food industry.**
  2. **Regulating marketing and improving consumer information** is more effective than outright product bans.
- Replacing even **10% of UPF with minimally processed whole food** produces measurable improvements in cancer risk, metabolic health, and mortality outcomes.
- The goal is not individual willpower — it is **systemic change**: to the food environment, to regulation, and to the transparency of the industry.

> *"We must take back control of our diets and prioritize whole, nourishing foods."*
